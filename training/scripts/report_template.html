1. How long the robot stays on its feet

TensorBoard name: Train/mean_episode_length, for the Walker and its predecessors. Each practice round lasts up to 20 seconds, with shoves and thrown boxes throughout.

What it meansThe line is the average time a robot lasts before falling. The top of the chart, 20 seconds, means it survived the whole round. Higher is better.
Where it is nowRobots now last about 19.4 of 20 seconds on average while being kicked every few seconds. The two sharp dips are not the robot getting worse. Training was stopped and restarted there to add a new skill, and the counter begins again from short rounds before catching up within a few dozen rounds.

2. How well it follows walking and turning instructions

TensorBoard names: rew/tracking_lin_vel and rew/tracking_ang_vel, shown as a share of the best possible score.

Walking speed and directionTurning
What it meansThe robot is told "go this fast, this way, and turn this quickly". 100% would mean it matches the instruction exactly at every instant. Because a walking body sways with each step, a very good walker scores well below 100%.
Where it is nowWalking reached about 82% early and stayed there. Turning was stuck near 33%: the robot was ignoring turn instructions. After a reward for facing the right way was added, turning climbed to 47%. In a direct test it now turns at 0.69 when asked for 0.8, up from zero.

3. How often the attacker knocks the opponent off

TensorBoard name: rew/win, converted to knock-outs per minute of sparring, for two versions of the attacker.

Opponent stands its ground at the rimOpponent keeps walking back to the centre
What it meansEach point is how many times per minute the learning robot shoved its opponent off the summit. Zero means every round ended in a stalemate.
Where it is nowAgainst an opponent that just stands near the edge, the attacker went from almost never winning to more than 20 knock-outs a minute, and wins 98.7% of test rounds. Against an opponent that walks back to the centre the line never left zero: two identical robots have the same grip and strength, so a plain shove is not enough.

In plain terms

  • The basics are solid. One brain, the Walker, can stand through hard shoves, walk and turn on command, and walk up to another robot. All of that behaves the same in Unity as in training, to within a few centimetres after walking two metres.
  • Everyone now starts on the summit. A version called the Climber did learn to walk back up the slope, in training and in Unity, but climbing has been set aside for now.
  • Fighting works, improving it is the current job. The Attacker shoves a standing robot off the summit in about two seconds. When two Attackers meet, 82% of rounds end with one of them knocked out, typically in about 5 seconds. Seven generations were trained, each against the earlier ones. The latest wins 80% of its decided rounds against the previous five.
  • One fighting brain now covers every combat test. The All-rounder began as the early Attacker and then learned to duel while still practising against standing robots, so it kept both skills. Movement tests still use the Walker, which has no opponent inputs.
  • Unity now has the game shell. A fighter-select menu, a match camera that follows the loser, the rising sea, a score overlay and synthesized impact sounds. The picture was checked from captured frames; the sound has not been listened to.
  • Not done: the victory dance, which was set aside at the start.
Worth knowing. In a control test, the standing opponent wandered off the edge by itself in about 10% of 15-second rounds with nobody touching it. The attacker's wins come in about 2 seconds, far faster than that drift, but the true figure for "wins caused by the attacker" is a little below the 98.7% shown. In Unity the attacker's inputs and brain match training exactly. Fights cannot be compared move for move because each robot reacts to the other, so 60 Unity rounds were compared with 200 training rounds instead: 53% decided in both, typical win at 3.3 s in both.

Sources: evaluation runs with 256 to 512 robots and 3 random seeds each (training/scripts/eval.py), training logs under training/runs, and Unity comparison files under training/assets/g1. Full history in rl_optimization_log.md.
